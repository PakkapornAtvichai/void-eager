# Evidence to Mechanics

## 1. Autism-specific target evidence

### Lage et al. — cognitive flexibility meta-analysis
- 59 studies; 2122 autistic participants without intellectual disability and 2036 comparison participants.
- Small-to-moderate group difference in cognitive flexibility.
- Substantial heterogeneity.
- Design implication: target an experience profile, not an autism severity band.
- This paper does not prescribe a game loop or clinical intervention.

### Buckle et al. — autistic inertia
- Qualitative focus groups with 32 autistic adults.
- Participants described difficulty starting, stopping, and changing activities.
- Helpful support included compatible environmental activity, practical prompting, continuity, and lowering the threshold to act.
- The authors recommend avoiding unnecessary interruptions when continuity is desired.
- Conventional alarms/lists/reminders/calendars were often reported as unhelpful.
- Design implication: do not claim a note/pin tool itself treats autistic inertia.
## 2. Interruption/resumption mechanism

### Trafton et al. 2003
- Primary task already underway before interruption.
- Interruption warning created an interruption lag before the secondary task began.
- An 8-second warning condition led participants to prepare more and resume faster than immediate interruption.
- Mechanism: prospective goal encoding / rehearsal before switching.

### Altmann & Trafton 2004
- Primary task was complex planning/resource allocation.
- Secondary task lasted roughly 30–45 seconds.
- Interruption timing was system-controlled, not user-triggered.
- External cues available during the interruption lag supported later resumption.
- Resumption lag was measured after returning to the primary task.

### Design implication
Our interruption should arrive asynchronously while the player is cognitively engaged. The player should have a short preparation window in which a Pin can encode "where I am / what I was about to do" before switching.
## 3. Autism serious-game / EF intervention structure

### Faja et al. 2022
- Web-based EF games plus in-person metacognition coaching.
- Sessions combined repeated game practice with explicit EF strategy coaching.
- Consistent language and visual supports were used.
- Coaching asked questions such as what strategy the child used and what they could do differently.
- Findings supported feasibility and some neural/repetitive-behavior changes, but did not show broad EF generalization.

### Caribbean Quest
- Autism-specific pilot serious game.
- Hybrid process-specific + compensatory approach.
- Repetitive, hierarchically graded, adaptive exercises.
- Gameplay accompanied by adult-delivered metacognitive strategy instruction.

### Braingame Brian / ASD RCT
- Adaptive EF training embedded in a game world.
- Working-memory, inhibition, and cognitive-flexibility tasks repeated across sessions.
- Difficulty adapted to performance.
- The ASD RCT did not show clear differential benefit over mock training; high attrition cautions against assuming game mechanics alone transfer to daily life.
## 4. Proposed Void-eager translation

The game should not provide immediate truth feedback for mystery conclusions.

Instead:
- interruptions are system-timed while the player is already reading/searching;
- a brief pre-switch window allows context encoding;
- Quick Pin minimizes into a persistent retrieval cue;
- the secondary task occupies attention before the player can freely resume;
- after interruption, the player chooses what to do next rather than being auto-returned;
- hypotheses can remain wrong and influence later interpretation or final case outcome;
- feedback focuses on strategy/process at reflection, not instant narrative correctness;
- at least two active investigation threads should coexist so context management has value.

This is a research-informed design hypothesis, not a validated clinical protocol.
